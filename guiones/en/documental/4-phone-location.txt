# Satellites that listen
@energy 4
> Explain with confidence, step by step.

The answer is about twenty thousand kilometers above your head.
Around thirty satellites of the **global** positioning system orbit up there.
Each one circles the Earth twice a day.
They're arranged so there are always several above any place on the planet.
Each satellite sends, nonstop, a signal with its position and the exact time.
Your phone sends nothing: it only **listens**.
[pause]
When it receives several signals, it measures how long each one took to arrive.
Since they travel at the speed of light, that time becomes distance.
With four satellites, it can work out where you are and your **altitude**.
All of that happens in a fraction of a second.
And you don't have to do a thing!

# Clocks of precision
@energy 4
> Here comes the surprising fact: give it weight.

For this to work, the time has to be **perfect**.
That's why every satellite carries atomic clocks on board.
These clocks keep time with amazing accuracy.
An error of one microsecond would move you about three hundred meters.
And there's an even stranger detail.
[pause]
Clocks in orbit run a tiny bit **faster** than clocks on Earth.
Einstein's theory of relativity explains why.
Nobody would notice it in daily life, but the system does.
If no one corrected that difference, the error would grow by about ten **kilometers** a day.
So every time you use a map, you're also using advanced physics.
Who would have thought Einstein helps you get to the bakery? [up]

# It's not alone
@energy 4
> Brisk pace, with the list clearly marked.

The United States system isn't the only one in the sky.
Europe has Galileo, Russia has Glonass{glo-NASS} and China has BeiDou{bay-DOH}.
Many phones listen to **several** systems at once.
That way they get more signals and a more precise location.
On an open street, the error can be just a **few** meters.
But among tall buildings, your location sometimes jumps from one street to another.
The signals bounce off walls and arrive late.
[breathe]
And inside a building, the roof and brick walls block the signals.
Then your phone asks for **help**.
It uses nearby Wi-Fi networks and cell towers.
With that, it estimates a rough position, though less exact.

# Protect your battery and your privacy
@energy 4
> Practical tone, like advice between friends.

Calculating your position all the time drains your **battery**.
That's why it's worth checking which apps use your location.
Many ask for it even when they don't need it.
[pause]
In the settings you can choose: always, only while using the app, or never.
That choice also protects your **privacy**.
Your location reveals where you live, where you work and where you walk.
It's valuable information, so share it with **care**.
You can also switch location off when you're not using it.
Your battery and your peace of mind will thank you.
A small change today can save you more than one problem tomorrow.
Check your permissions this week!
