# A sacred drink
@energy 4
> A clear, measured report.

The peoples of Mesoamerica were among the first to prepare cacao.
The Maya drank it as a thick, **bitter** drink topped with foam.
Sometimes they added chili, vanilla or flowers.
Some accounts from that time describe how it was poured from one jar to another to make it froth.
For the Aztecs, cacao seeds were a true treasure, used even as **money**.
With them, people could pay for food, clothing and other goods at the market.
The drink was reserved above all for nobles, warriors and ceremonies.
So how did it reach the rest of the world? [up]
The answer lies in the ships that crossed the Atlantic in the **sixteenth** century.
[pause]

# Arrival in Europe
@energy 4
> Keep the newscast rhythm.

In the sixteenth century, cacao reached Spain, and from there it spread across Europe.
Europeans made one **key** change: they added sugar.
They also began to drink it hot, sometimes with cinnamon.
For a long time, it was a luxury drink in royal courts and palaces.
In some places, people even debated whether drinking it broke a religious fast.
Chocolate houses became fashionable in cities like London in the seventeenth century.
The price was still **high**, because all the cacao came from the Americas.
Over the years, cacao farming spread to other tropical regions.
That shift set the stage for the **great** transformation that came next.
[pause]

# From cup to bar
@energy 4
> The central story: firm and precise.

The big news came in the nineteenth century, with the machines of the industrial age.
In eighteen twenty-eight, a press was invented that separated cocoa butter from the cacao.
Thanks to that invention, chocolate could **harden** and be sold in bars.
By the middle of that century, the first solid bars for eating appeared.
Shortly after, in Switzerland, a chocolate maker had the idea of mixing it with **milk**.
That's how milk chocolate was born, smoother and sweeter.
Factories began producing it in huge quantities.
That was the beginning of the chocolate industry as we know it.
Prices dropped, and chocolate stopped being a luxury.
The result? [up]
A product that is now sold in almost every **corner** of the planet.
[pause]

# Cacao today
@energy 4
> Newscast close: calm.

To close, a look at the present.
Today, most cacao is not grown in the Americas but in West Africa.
Ivory Coast and Ghana together produce more than **half** of the world's cacao.
The cacao tree only grows in warm, humid areas near the equator.
Millions of small family farms depend on this crop.
Cacao is harvested by hand, opening each pod with care to remove the seeds.
That's why more and more people are asking how **much** cacao farmers are paid.
From a sacred drink to a bar on a supermarket shelf, it has been a long journey.
And the history of chocolate is still being **written**.
